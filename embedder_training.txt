Let's talk about how embedder models are trained. When you use a chat model like GPT or Claude, you're talking to a generative transformer, the kind we've discussed before. But quietly running alongside those flashy models is a whole other class of transformer, the embedder. Embedders are the workhorses of semantic search, of retrieval-augmented generation, of clustering, of duplicate detection, of almost any task where you need to compare two pieces of text by meaning rather than by exact words. And they're trained in a way that's actually quite different from how generative models are trained. That's what I want to walk through today, step by step.

Let's start with what an embedder actually is. An embedder is a model that takes a piece of text, anywhere from a single word to a long document, and produces a fixed-length vector of numbers. The vector is usually somewhere between three hundred and four thousand numbers long. That's it. That's the whole output. There's no next token, no generated text, no probability distribution. Just one vector per input.

The magic isn't in the vector itself. It's in what the vector space means. An embedder is trained so that texts with similar meanings produce nearby vectors, and texts with different meanings produce distant vectors. You measure similarity with cosine similarity, which is basically the angle between two vectors, or with dot product. Once you have that, you can do remarkable things. You can take a user's question, embed it, compare it against a database of embedded documents, and find the most semantically relevant ones. You can cluster a million customer reviews by topic without ever having to define the topics in advance. You can detect duplicates across different phrasings. The embedder is, in a sense, a machine that compresses meaning into geometry.

Now, how do we train one? Our next topic is the core training goal, because once you understand the goal, the rest follows naturally. The goal is this. Given a training example of two pieces of text that ought to be similar, the model's job is to make their vectors close together. Given two pieces of text that ought to be different, the model's job is to make their vectors far apart. Every technical decision in embedder training is in service of this one simple idea.

But before we can train, we need a starting point, and we need a way to turn text into a vector at all. That brings us to architecture. Almost every modern embedder starts from a pretrained transformer, usually an encoder-style one like BERT, though increasingly decoder-style transformers are also used. The encoder has already been trained on a huge amount of text using some form of masked language modeling, so it already knows about grammar, about word meanings, about context. We're not starting from scratch. We're taking a model that already has a rich understanding of language and teaching it to produce useful summary vectors.

The tricky part is that a transformer outputs one vector per input token. If you feed it a sentence with twenty tokens, you get back twenty vectors. But we need a single vector to represent the whole sentence. So we need to pool. There are a few ways to do this. The simplest, and the one BERT was originally designed for, is to use the special CLS token. BERT adds a special token called CLS at the beginning of every input, and the representation of that token at the top layer is treated as the summary vector. The model learns during training to stuff meaningful summary information into that slot.

The other popular approach is mean pooling. You just take the average of all the output token vectors. This tends to work surprisingly well, sometimes better than CLS pooling, because every token gets to contribute to the final vector. Some implementations also use max pooling, or attention pooling where a small learned mechanism decides how to weight the tokens. The choice of pooling strategy isn't decisive, but it matters, and different embedder families make different choices.

After pooling, many embedders add a small projection head, which is usually a couple of linear layers that map the pooled vector to a final embedding dimension. This is sometimes just an identity, sometimes it's used to reduce dimensionality, and sometimes it's a key part of the training setup. Finally, the output is usually L2 normalized, meaning it's divided by its own length so that every vector has magnitude one. This matters because once vectors are unit length, dot product and cosine similarity become the same thing, which simplifies a lot of downstream math.

Now we get to the interesting part. How does the training actually work? The dominant technique is called contrastive learning, and it's worth understanding clearly because it shows up everywhere in modern machine learning. The core idea is that you need positive pairs and negative pairs. A positive pair is two pieces of text that belong together, that mean similar things. A negative pair is two pieces of text that don't belong together.

Where do positive pairs come from? This is one of the interesting practical questions. You can't easily generate them with humans at scale, so researchers have gotten clever about finding them in the wild. Question-and-answer pairs from places like Stack Exchange and Reddit give you millions of naturally occurring positive pairs. A question and its accepted answer are semantically related by construction. Wikipedia article titles and their opening paragraphs work the same way. The title is a compressed description of the content. Research papers give you title-abstract pairs, or citation pairs where two papers that cite each other are likely about related topics. Search logs from web engines, when available, give you query-click pairs, the gold standard since they encode what a human actually thought was relevant.

Once you have positive pairs, you also need negative pairs. Here's where the first big trick shows up, called in-batch negatives. Instead of explicitly curating negative pairs, you use the other examples in your training batch. Imagine a batch of one hundred and twenty eight positive pairs. For any given pair, the positive is its matching partner, and the negatives are the one hundred and twenty seven other second-elements in the batch. This is cheap and effective, because almost any other randomly chosen text is probably not a good match for your query. You get one hundred and twenty seven negatives for free with every positive.

Now, the objective function. The standard contrastive loss is called InfoNCE, which stands for noise contrastive estimation, and the same idea is sometimes called NT-Xent, the normalized temperature-scaled cross-entropy loss. The math looks intimidating but the idea is simple. For each anchor query, you compute its similarity to every candidate in the batch. You scale those similarities by a temperature parameter, usually called tau, and apply softmax. The loss is the negative log probability of the correct positive. In plain language, the model is being asked a multiple choice question. Here's a query. Which of these one hundred and twenty eight candidates is its true partner? Pick one. The loss pushes the probability of the right answer up and everything else down.

The temperature parameter matters a lot. A low temperature, say 0.05, makes the softmax very peaky, which forces the model to really discriminate between similar candidates. A higher temperature, say 0.2, makes the softmax softer, which is more forgiving and tends to train more stably but with less final sharpness. Most production embedders use temperatures in the range of 0.02 to 0.1. It's one of the most important hyperparameters in embedder training and people tune it carefully.

Notice what this loss is actually doing geometrically. Every training step, it's pulling the positive pair closer together in vector space, and pushing all the other pairs in the batch apart. After millions of steps, you end up with a space where semantically similar texts have been dragged together and semantically different texts have been pushed apart. The geometry of the final embedding space is the accumulated residue of all those pulls and pushes.

This is also why batch size matters enormously for contrastive learning. Because your negatives come from inside the batch, a bigger batch means more negatives per step, which means a stronger training signal. Small batches of sixty four or one hundred and twenty eight are okay. Large batches of eight thousand or even sixty four thousand produce dramatically better embedders. This is why the best embedder models are often trained on large clusters with aggressive memory optimization. They need those huge batches to get competitive quality.

Now that we understand the core mechanism, let's walk through the actual stages of training a modern embedder, because it's not one single training run. It's a pipeline of multiple stages, each doing something different.

Our next topic is stage one, which is often called weakly supervised contrastive pretraining. In this stage, the goal is to take a pretrained language model and teach it roughly where meanings cluster. The dataset here is enormous and noisy. Hundreds of millions to billions of pairs, mined from the open web with minimal filtering. Reddit post titles paired with their bodies. Stack Exchange questions paired with top answers. Wikipedia titles paired with first paragraphs. Academic paper titles paired with abstracts. Product titles paired with descriptions. All of this is stitched together into one giant pile of weak positive pairs.

The pairs are noisy. Many of them are imperfect. Some are outright bad. But because the dataset is huge and diverse, the model can still learn the general shape of semantic space. It learns that questions about cooking should land near answers about cooking. It learns that Spanish text about soccer should land near English text about soccer. It learns the broad contours of meaning across languages and domains. This stage might run for hundreds of thousands of training steps, with batch sizes in the thousands, and it produces a model that's already pretty good at general semantic similarity.

Now our next topic is stage two, supervised fine-tuning. This is where we take that roughly-trained model and sharpen it on high-quality labeled data. The datasets here are much smaller, usually a few hundred thousand to a few million pairs, but they're cleaner. Popular sources include MS MARCO, which is a huge collection of Bing query-document pairs, Natural Questions from Google, the SNLI and MultiNLI datasets for sentence similarity, and various domain-specific collections. In this stage, the pairs are specifically designed for the task you care about. For retrieval embedders, that means query-document pairs. For sentence similarity, it means sentence pairs with similarity labels.

This stage is where the model becomes good at specific tasks. The pretraining gave it general semantic sense. The fine-tuning gives it the specific behavior we need. A well-fine-tuned embedder on MS MARCO, for example, becomes specifically good at ranking passages for search queries. Without this stage, the model is a jack of all trades but master of none.

Our next topic is a specific technique that's critical here, called hard negative mining. I mentioned earlier that in-batch negatives are cheap and easy. The problem is that they're often too easy. If your anchor is a query about Python programming, and one of your random in-batch negatives is a recipe for lasagna, the model can tell them apart instantly. That negative teaches the model very little. The really informative negatives are the ones that are almost right. The Python question paired with a JavaScript answer. The medical query paired with a veterinary document. These are called hard negatives, and they're where the real learning happens.

How do you get hard negatives? You mine them. Here's the typical workflow. You take your current embedder, however well-trained it already is. You take every query in your training set. You embed it, and you search the full corpus of documents for the top one hundred most similar. Then you look at those top hundred. Your true positive is presumably somewhere in there. Everything else in that top hundred is, by the current model's judgment, a highly plausible match that happens to not be the real answer. Those are your hard negatives. You add them to the training set, and you train another round.

This is an iterative process. Each round, the model gets better. Its judgment of "hard" becomes more refined. So in the next round, you mine hard negatives again, using the newly improved model. You might do this three or four times. Each iteration squeezes more performance out of the same dataset. Some of the best embedders in the world right now, like the ones from the BGE family, from E5, from Jina, all use multi-round hard negative mining as a core part of their recipe.

There's also been recent work on using large language models to generate hard negatives directly. You ask an LLM to take a query and produce a passage that looks relevant but is subtly wrong. These synthetic hard negatives are a growing area of research and are starting to show up in frontier embedder training.

Our next topic is stage three, which is sometimes a task-specific fine-tuning stage. If you know you're going to use the embedder for retrieval on legal documents, or for clustering scientific papers, or for code search, you can do one final round of fine-tuning on a small amount of data from that specific domain. Because the model already has strong general capability from the earlier stages, this last fine-tune can be done with relatively little data and still produce significant gains. Some production systems do this, others don't, depending on whether they have labeled domain data.

Now let's step back and talk about evaluation, because this is where a lot of subtlety hides. How do you know if your embedder is good? The dominant benchmark in the field is MTEB, the Massive Text Embedding Benchmark. MTEB aggregates dozens of tasks into a single leaderboard. Retrieval, classification, clustering, reranking, pair classification, summarization similarity, and semantic textual similarity. Each task measures a different aspect of what embeddings are for.

Here's the important insight. These tasks don't all want the same thing. A retrieval task wants embeddings that are precisely calibrated for query-document matching. A classification task wants embeddings that linearly separate class labels. A clustering task wants embeddings where intuitive groups form tight clusters. These requirements are related but they're not identical. An embedder that's amazing at retrieval might be merely okay at clustering. An embedder that's tuned specifically for semantic textual similarity might do worse on retrieval because those tasks emphasize different aspects of meaning.

So there's no single best embedder. There are embedders that are best on average across MTEB, but your specific use case might be better served by a different one. This is also why modern embedder releases often include several sizes and variants. A small fast one for when you need throughput, a big accurate one for when you need precision, and maybe a specialized one for code or for multilingual work.

Let's close with a few modern wrinkles that are reshaping how embedders are trained. Our next topic is Matryoshka embeddings. The idea here is clever. Instead of training the model to produce one fixed embedding dimension, you train it so that the first few dimensions of the output are themselves a valid, shorter embedding. So a model might produce a four thousand dimensional vector, but the first two thousand dimensions are also a usable embedding, and the first one thousand also works, and the first five hundred also works, all the way down. You do this by computing the contrastive loss not just on the full vector, but on truncated versions as well, and averaging. The result is a model where you can trade off quality for speed at inference time just by truncating the vector. This is extremely useful in production, where storage and compute costs scale linearly with dimension.

Our next topic is instruction-tuned embeddings. The idea is that the same piece of text might need to be embedded differently depending on what you're going to do with it. A query "climate change" embedded for retrieval is a different thing from the same query embedded for classification. Instruction-tuned embedders take this seriously by accepting a natural language instruction as part of their input. You might prepend something like "represent this sentence for retrieving related Wikipedia articles" and the model uses that instruction to shape the embedding. This makes the same model usable across many tasks without retraining. The E5 and instructor families pioneered this.

Finally, our next topic is late interaction, exemplified by ColBERT. All the embedders we've discussed produce a single vector per document. ColBERT takes a different approach. It keeps one vector per token, and at query time it computes similarity by matching each query token to its best matching document token, and summing. This is more expressive than a single-vector comparison, because it lets the model express the idea that some parts of the document are relevant to some parts of the query, while other parts aren't. The cost is more storage and more computation at query time, since you're storing and comparing many more vectors. ColBERT-style models sit in a middle ground between fast single-vector retrieval and slow but accurate cross-encoder reranking.

Let's tie this all together. We started with a goal. Train a model that maps text to vectors where similar meanings are close and different meanings are far apart. We took a pretrained transformer and added a pooling step to collapse token vectors into a single vector. We then trained it using contrastive learning, with positive pairs from naturally occurring sources and negatives from within the batch. We used the InfoNCE loss to pull positives together and push negatives apart, with a temperature parameter that shapes how sharply. We ran a multi-stage pipeline. Weak contrastive pretraining on billions of noisy pairs, then supervised fine-tuning on cleaner data, then hard negative mining to sharpen the hardest cases, and possibly one final task-specific fine-tune. We evaluated on a benchmark like MTEB, knowing that different tasks want different things. And we layered on modern techniques like Matryoshka training for flexible dimensions, instruction tuning for task adaptability, and late interaction for fine-grained matching.

Every step was a decision. Every decision had a tradeoff. Bigger batches give better negatives but cost more memory. Harder negatives give better discrimination but are expensive to mine. Larger dimensions give richer embeddings but more storage. Instruction tuning gives flexibility but adds complexity. The embedder you end up with is the accumulated result of all those choices, just like the transformers we discussed before.

The next time you use a semantic search, or a RAG system, or any system that compares text by meaning, there's an embedder somewhere underneath doing the work, and it got to be good at its job through this exact pipeline. A pretrained language model, taught by contrast, refined by mining, and evaluated against a dozen different notions of what "good" means. That's how you train a machine to understand meaning well enough to put it on a map.
